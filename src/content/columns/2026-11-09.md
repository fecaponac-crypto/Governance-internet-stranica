The development that matters most this week is a fine for conduct that ended in 2020. Norway's Datatilsynet has [fined Microsoft-owned Xandr NOK 92 million](/news/norway-s-datatilsynet-fines-microsoft-owned-xandr-nok-92-million-over/) for passing data about Grindr users through real-time bidding auctions. It is the largest fine the regulator has ever imposed, and Datatilsynet says Xandr has not appealed and the decision is final.

The size is not the point for most of us. The reasoning is. Xandr argued that it believed Grindr had collected consent. Datatilsynet found the two were joint controllers and that Xandr never verified whether that consent was valid. Being a Grindr user indicates sexual orientation, so explicit consent was required. The regulator's line is that each company must itself ensure, and be able to prove, that it may use the data.

For practitioners, this lands on every inbound data feed where the lawful basis lives in someone else's system. A clause in a data-sharing agreement saying "the partner warrants consent has been obtained" is a promise, not evidence. My read is that governance teams should treat inherited lawful bases as a data quality attribute: something you can test, sample and report on, not just record.

There is a classification lesson too. The decision treats the mere fact of using a service as special-category data. Metadata about which app someone uses can carry the same sensitivity as the attribute it implies.

## Show it works

The same demand for proof runs through the week's other stories.

The European Commission has [preliminarily found TikTok's 'For You' feed in breach of the DSA](/news/european-commission-preliminarily-finds-tiktok-s-for-you-algorithm-bre/) over "rabbit hole" effects. The finding is not about content removal. It says the risk assessment was inadequate and the mitigations do not work: the "not interested" button may not change the feed because the algorithm prioritises viewing time. A mitigation that exists on paper but not in the outputs counts for little.

The US OCC's [examiner framework for AI and agentic systems](/news/occ-gives-bank-examiners-a-16-component-framework-for-supervising-ai-a/) points the same way. Its data governance component reaches into the origin and licensing of training data, including data embedded in third-party foundation models, and into new data created by prompts, outputs, embeddings and logs. The [AMA's agentic AI framework](/news/ama-publishes-governance-framework-for-agentic-ai-taking-on-clinical-f/) says clinical authority should be "earned through evidence and maintained through demonstrated performance".

The CJEU Advocate General's [opinion in Like Company](/news/cjeu-advocate-general-says-ai-chatbots-reusing-press-articles-in-real/) fits the pattern. Providers would be liable where reuse of press articles is inherent to the business model or where they have not taken credible, effective countermeasures. I think the practical consequence, if the Court follows him, is that training and real-time retrieval become two separate rows in the AI risk register, with retrieval needing evidence that paywalls and scraping restrictions were respected.

The CNIL's [final recommendation on session replay tools](/news/cnil-publishes-final-recommendation-on-session-replay-tools-confirming/) makes the same move at a smaller scale. No consent exemption, purposes fixed before deployment, and masking of irrelevant data, of mouse movements where authentication codes are entered, and of chatbot conversations. Masking becomes a configuration you can inspect, not a default you hope is on.

## Brussels pulls the other way

Against this, the Council has [agreed its Digital Omnibus negotiating mandate](/news/eu-council-agrees-digital-omnibus-negotiating-mandate-including-gdpr-p/). According to noyb, the Council's Article 88bis would presume a legitimate interest for processing personal data "in the context of AI" and narrow the definition of personal data for pseudonymised data. The mandate also repeals the Data Governance Act and the Open Data Directive.

My read is that this creates a real tension. Enforcement is asking for more proof per controller; the legislative direction may lower the bar for the lawful basis itself. Parliament has yet to take its position, so nothing changes today. But DPOs should know now which AI use cases rest on consent or a case-by-case legitimate interest assessment, and how pseudonymised identifiers are classified in the catalogue. Those are the entries that would move.

## Exemptions get narrower

Two enforcement stories trim shields that organisations assumed protected them. Sweden's IMY [reprimanded four people-search services](/news/imy-reprimands-four-swedish-people-search-services-and-rules-gdpr-over/), finding that the Swedish exemptions attached to voluntary publishing certificates go further than EU law allows and applying Articles 10 and 17. IMY reports around 3,800 complaints against the companies. In Berlin, according to noyb, the data protection authority [reprimanded four parties over 2021 microtargeting](/news/berlin-data-protection-authority-reprimands-four-german-parties-over-2/) for lacking a legal basis such as consent; we have not seen the decisions directly.

The CNIL's [guidance on internal research by health professionals](/news/cnil-sets-out-gdpr-duties-for-internal-research-by-health-professional/) is a gentler version: no prior formalities, but records, possible DPIAs, patient information and the right to object still apply, and pseudonymised data is not anonymised data.
