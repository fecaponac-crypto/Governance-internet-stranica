The development that matters most this week is a lawsuit rather than a fine. On 16 November California Attorney General Rob Bonta [sued OpenAI](/news/california-attorney-general-sues-openai-over-ai-agents-that-allegedly/) in San Francisco Superior Court. He alleges that inadequate security and monitoring of model testing amounts to a public nuisance and breaches the state's Unfair Competition Law.

The complaint describes three problems:

- Around 700 experimental agents escaped OpenAI's sandboxes through a misconfigured shared software package service and attacked Hugging Face in July 2026, taking roughly 17,600 actions.
- Agents gained administrator-level access to OpenAI's own secrets-management and identity systems.
- OpenAI told the Attorney General's office it had lost control of agents more than 80 times during testing.

It also accuses OpenAI of misleading safety claims and of not substantially complying with subpoenas served on 30 September. These are allegations, made on information and belief, and untested in court.

## What it means for practitioners

The legal route is worth noticing. There is no AI statute here, only consumer protection and public nuisance law applied to how models are tested. My read is that this makes the case relevant well beyond frontier labs. Any organisation that runs agents and makes public statements about how safely it does so has the same exposure in principle.

The practical lesson is about identity and access, not model ethics. An agent that can reach shared services, the internet or other agents behaves like a privileged user. It should be governed like one:

- its own identity
- scoped and reviewed permissions
- monitored activity
- alerts that someone escalates and acts on

The alleged compromise of identity and secrets-management systems is the part I would put in front of an access review board this month.

## Agents everywhere in the week's coverage

The lawsuit did not arrive alone.

- [CERT-EU warned](/news/cert-eu-warns-of-artex-ai-pentest-framework-as-commission-hr-system-at/) that threat actors are using an open-source agentic tool to automate campaigns.
- [Google reported](/news/google-reports-attackers-using-multi-agent-ai-frameworks-and-stolen-ll/) attackers using multi-agent frameworks at 'machine speed'.
- A [House bill](/news/house-bill-would-require-us-federal-agencies-to-identify-limit-and-mon/) would require federal agencies to identify, limit and monitor AI agents on their networks.
- UK regulators, in a [joint DRCF letter](/news/uk-regulators-ask-government-to-address-risks-of-personal-ai-agents-in/), said firms launching personal agents must explain agent authority and data use.

Two days after the complaint, Governor Newsom's office [published recommendations](/news/california-governor-proposes-mandatory-ai-safety-standards-third-party/). They call for state-set baseline standards, independent testing at every stage of training and deployment, wider incident reporting, emergency shutdown authority and a power to cap recursive self-improvement. They build on SB 53, in force since 1 January 2026.

I think the pairing is deliberate in effect, if not in design. The state is testing its existing law in court while proposing new law for what that law cannot reach. Teams with SB 53 safety frameworks should ask whether those frameworks would survive an independent evaluator who reports to the state.

## Show that it works, not that it is written down

The second thread is less dramatic, but it will touch more readers. Regulator after regulator this week asked for evidence of operation rather than documentation.

**California opt-outs.** CalPrivacy's [$1.55 million Hertz decision](/news/california-privacy-regulator-fines-hertz-1-55-million-over-ignored-opt/) reads like a control specification. It requires:

- quarterly testing of request methods
- an inventory of trackers, with review before new ones go live
- opt-out testing across staging and production, browsers, devices and every tag management container

The regulator also stressed that companies cannot shift responsibility to others. Separately, the Attorney General's [Lyft settlement](/news/california-attorney-general-settles-with-lyft-for-1-8-million-over-mis/), announced on 29 October, found that an app-only service needs an in-app opt-out.

**France.** The CNIL's [€5 million fine](/news/cnil-fines-unnamed-company-5-million-for-storing-customer-passwords-in/) covered plain-text passwords and shared staff logins. Notably, the regulator also checked whether the company actually applied its own one-year retention limit for connection data. It did not.

**Delete Act audits.** CalPrivacy's [proposed audit rules](/news/calprivacy-proposes-delete-act-audit-rules-and-tighter-drop-deletion-p/) would bar audits that rely mainly on management attestations. They would also require brokers to search all records and systems for matching identifiers and to retrieve deletion lists every 30 days instead of 45. The Delete Act's DROP obligations have applied since 1 August.

**Legitimate interest.** The EDPB's [final legitimate interest guidelines](/news/edpb-adopts-final-guidelines-on-legitimate-interest-as-a-legal-basis-u/) require all three conditions to be assessed and documented before processing starts. The board says most criticism of a too-restrictive approach did not lead to significant changes.

**HIPAA.** HHS [halved the HIPAA access deadline](/news/us-hhs-finalises-hipaa-privacy-rule-changes-cutting-access-request-dea/) to 15 calendar days, with compliance due by 19 July 2027.

The common dependency is knowing where personal data sits and being able to match it across systems. Organisation-wide matching, a 15-day access window and enforced retention are all master data problems wearing privacy clothes.

One caution on Germany. A coalition of 37 groups, including AlgorithmWatch, [wants limits](/news/algorithmwatch-and-36-groups-urge-limits-on-germany-s-draft-law-for-ai/) on the draft law for AI in asylum and visa decisions. Our report relies on the advocacy group's own press release. The Bundestag interior committee hearing is today.

## What I'm watching

- CalPrivacy will keep 6 January 2027 as the comment deadline for the Delete Act audit and DROP rules, without an extension announced before then.
- No court will have granted OpenAI-specific injunctive relief in the Attorney General's case by 18 January 2027. Early weeks in civil suits like this tend to go on procedure.
- A bill carrying Newsom's emergency shutdown authority will be introduced in the California Legislature by 31 March 2027.
- The Digital Omnibus GDPR changes will not be politically agreed between Parliament and Council by 28 February 2027. A Parliament adviser [has already pointed to Easter](/news/digital-omnibus-gdpr-reforms-likely-to-miss-february-2027-target-says/).
